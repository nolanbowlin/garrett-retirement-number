This is better shape than most people ever get to, and the remaining upside is rarely about saving harder. In this range it comes from tax positioning, from when Social Security gets claimed, and from making sure nothing in the plan depends on a single thing going right. Two of those apply to you, and they are below.

Where the number is leaking

Yield, 12 out of 20. What pays you when the paychecks stop.

Almost everything in your plan comes out of savings. You have not compared your Social Security benefit at 62, at full retirement age and at 70, and there is no written order for which accounts get drawn down first. The gap between claiming at 62 and at 70 is roughly seventy seven percent more per month for life, and for a married couple the claiming decision is often worth more than another five years of saving. It costs nothing to fix and it is the cheapest ground on the board.

Dependence, 12.7 out of 20. How much rests on one thing going right.

The business is the single largest asset in the plan and the plan currently assumes it sells for what you think it is worth. If it came in thirty percent light on the day you needed it, the shortfall roughly doubles. Two things change that: knowing what a buyer would actually pay, which means having it valued rather than estimated, and making sure it can run without you, because a business that depends on the owner sells for less or does not sell at all.

What this was built on

Every figure is in today's dollars, so nothing here has been inflated and nothing needs discounting back. Your invested assets are assumed to grow at 5 percent a year above inflation until you stop, which is deliberately below a pure stock market assumption because almost nobody within fifteen years of retiring is holding only stocks.

Your number is the assets required to cover $122,000 a year of spending, after the $36,000 a year you told me is coming from Social Security, a pension or rent, at a withdrawal rate of 4.0 percent. That rate moves with how long the money has to last: retiring earlier means a lower rate and a larger number.

Your business was counted at $1,330,000, which is seventy percent of the $1.9M you estimated. The discount covers tax on the sale, transaction fees, and the gap that usually sits between what an owner believes the business is worth and what a buyer pays.
